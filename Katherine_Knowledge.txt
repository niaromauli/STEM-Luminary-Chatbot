Biography Summary

Quick Facts
* Full Name: Katherine Coleman Goble Johnson
* Birth date: August 26, 1918 (White Sulphur Springs, WV)
* Death date: February 24, 2020 (age 101 years, Newport News, VA)
* Hometown: White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia
* Occupation: NASA Mathematician, Computer Programmer, Mathematics Teacher
* Katherine Johnson was given the name Katherine Coleman on her birth. Johnson is her married name (from her second husband)
* Katherine's father was Joshua Mckinley Coleman, born in White Sulphur Springs on 18 December 1881 to Horace Coleman and Margaret Johnson.
* Katherine’s moher was Joylette Roberta Lowe, was born in 1887 in Caswell County, North Carolina and became a school teacher. 
* Katherine had two older brothers, Horace born about 1912 and Charles born about 1915. She also had an older sister, Margaret, born about 1913.


Early Life
Family Background:
* Her mother was a teacher, and her father was a farmer and janitor.
* Katherine’s parents were determined that their four children would complete college. This was an ambitious goal for a Black family in the South.


Early Interest in Mathematics:
* From a young age, Johnson counted everything and could easily solve mathematical equations. 
* Her intelligence and skill with numbers became apparent when she was a child.


Education
High School Education
* By the time she was 10 years old, she had started attending high school. 
* She attended West Virginia State High School and graduated from high school at age 14. 


College Education
* In 1937, at age 18, Johnson graduated with highest honours from West Virginia State College (now West Virginia State University), earning bachelor’s degrees in mathematics and French.
* Johnson was one of the first African Americans to enroll in the mathematics program at West Virginia University.



Career
________________


Early Career
Teaching Career
* After college, Johnson began teaching in elementary and high schools in Virginia and West Virginia. There, she met fellow educator Jimmie Goble. 
* In 1939, she was selected to be one of the first three African American students to enroll in a graduate program at West Virginia University. 
* Katherine loved high-level math, but she did not see the value in completing her graduate degree. She left West Virginia University after marrying James Goble and deciding to start a family.
* During summer break, Jimmie worked as a chauffeur and Katherine as a maid because their teaching salaries were not enough to cover their expenses.


NACA Career
* In 1953, Katherine Johnson joined the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) at Langley Research Center as a research mathematician.
* NACA hired women mathematicians, called “computers,” to perform complex calculations and analyze flight test data for engineers.
* Johnson worked in the West Area Computing Unit, a group of Black women mathematicians.
* During this time, NACA was segregated, and Black employees used separate bathrooms and dining areas.
* Johnson quickly gained recognition for her mathematical and problem-solving skills.
* She identified errors in engineering calculations, which earned the trust of her colleagues and allowed her to remain on important project teams.
* She calculated the flight trajectory for Alan Shepard, the first American astronaut to travel to space.
* In 1958, NACA incorporated into NASA, and workplace segregation officially ended.


NASA Career
* At NASA, Johnson worked on calculations that helped send astronauts into orbit and safely return them to Earth. She studied spacecraft flight paths and landing locations. 
* She also authored a report on orbital spaceflight calculations, becoming the first woman in her division to receive credit as an author of a research report.
* In 1962, astronaut John Glenn requested Johnson to manually verify computer calculations for his orbital flight. Johnson spent nearly two days checking the data by hand. Glenn successfully orbited Earth three times, confirming the accuracy of her work.
* Johnson contributed to the Apollo 11 Moon landing mission (1969) by helping calculate launch windows, lunar orbits, and spacecraft coordination.
* She later worked on the Space Shuttle program and early research related to potential missions to Mars.
* Retired from NASA in 1986 after more than 30 years of service.

Space Task Group

Mercury Program (1961–1963)
* Contributed to NASA’s first human spaceflight program, which aimed to send American astronauts into space and safely return them to Earth.
* Calculated the flight trajectory for Freedom 7 (1961), the spacecraft that carried astronaut Alan B. Shepard Jr., the first American in space.
* Her calculations helped determine launch timing, flight path, and safe landing location.


John Glenn Friendship 7 Mission (1962)
* NASA began using electronic computers to calculate orbital flight paths, but the technology was still new and sometimes unreliable.
* Astronaut John Glenn specifically requested Johnson to manually verify the computer calculations before his mission. Johnson spent nearly two days checking the numbers by hand.
* Her verified calculations helped Glenn successfully orbit Earth three times, making him the first American to orbit the planet.


Apollo 11 Moon Landing Mission (1969)
* Contributed to calculations that helped send astronauts to the Moon and safely return them to Earth.
* Helped determine:
   * Rocket launch windows
   * Spacecraft flight paths to lunar orbit
   * Timing and coordination between the lunar module and command module
* Her mathematical work supported the success of the first human Moon landing.


Space Shuttle Program
* Later in her career, Johnson worked on calculations related to reusable spacecraft missions.
* Helped develop mathematical models and trajectory calculations that supported shuttle flight safety and mission planning.
* Worked on early theoretical calculations related to future human spaceflight to Mars.




Research & Publications
* Authored & co-authored 26 scientific research reports during her NASA career.
* In 1960, co-authored a research paper on spacecraft orbital calculations.
* Became the first woman in her division credited as an author of a research report.
* Her research helped improve understanding of orbital mechanics and spacecraft flight paths.


Awards & Recognition
* Received NASA’s Lunar Spacecraft and Operations Group Achievement Award.
* Received NASA’s Apollo Group Achievement Award.
* Awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom (2015) by President Barack Obama
* In 2016, NASA named the Katherine G. Johnson Computational Research Facility in her honor.
* Featured in the 2016 book and film Hidden Figures, which highlighted the contributions of Black women mathematicians at NASA, including Johnson, Dorothy Vaughan, and Mary Jackson.

Personal Life
* Katherine Johnson married fellow teacher James “Jimmie” Goble in 1939 and then married James Johnson in 1959.
* Katherine and James Goble had three daughters, born in 1940, 1943, and 1944.
* In 1951, the Goble family’s home caught fire. Seeking a fresh start, they moved to Newport News, Virginia, where Katherine later found opportunities to work at NACA.
* In 1956, Jimmie Goble died from a brain tumor. After his death, Katherine focused on raising her daughters and continuing her career.
* In 1958, Katherine met James “Jim” Johnson, who supported her career and built a strong relationship with her children.
* Throughout her life, Katherine strongly valued education. She ensured that all three of her daughters graduated from college.
* After and during her career, she advocated for increased access to STEM education, especially for Black girls and young women.




Historical Context
Katherine Johnson grew up during a time of racial segregation in the United States, when Black and white Americans were required to attend separate schools, use separate public facilities, and often had unequal access to education and job opportunities.


In West Virginia, educational opportunities for Black students were limited. The local Black school near Johnson’s home had only two classrooms serving seven grade levels. To allow their children to receive a full high school education, the Coleman family moved 80 miles so Katherine could attend school.


During Johnson’s early career, many professional opportunities for Black women were restricted. Teaching was one of the few widely available career paths.


When Johnson applied to the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) in the early 1950s, the workplace was still segregated. NACA employed two separate groups of female mathematicians, called “computers”: one group for white women and another for Black women, known as the West Area Computers.


Black computers were often required to meet higher hiring standards, including college degrees and strong academic records. 


Johnson challenged segregation in subtle but meaningful ways. She refused to follow separate bathroom rules, avoided segregated dining areas, and built professional relationships with white colleagues. She also insisted on attending important engineering briefings, which women were often excluded from at the time.


Johnson’s career took place during the Cold War and Space Race, a period when the United States and the Soviet Union competed to achieve major milestones in space exploration.

How Her Work Shows Up Today
________________


1.   Human Orbital Trajectory Planning
What Already Existed
The laws of motion (Newton) and planetary motion (Kepler) were established centuries earlier.
What She Helped Pioneer
Johnson helped translate those theoretical laws into practical calculations for the first U.S. human spaceflights. During missions such as Friendship 7, she calculated launch windows, orbital paths, and re-entry coordinates that enabled astronauts to safely orbit Earth.
This was pioneering in application: humans had never orbited Earth before.
How It Shows Up Today
Modern satellite launches, lunar missions, and Mars trajectories still rely on the same orbital mechanics principles — now executed by advanced software instead of slide rules.

2. Safe Re-Entry Calculations
What Already Existed
Physics governing atmospheric drag and gravitational motion were already known.
What She Helped Pioneer
Johnson helped calculate safe Earth re-entry paths for early missions, including work connected to Apollo 11 and contingency work supporting Apollo 13.
During this era, re-entry modeling for crewed spacecraft was still being operationally refined.
How It Shows Up Today
Modern spacecraft such as Crew Dragon use updated computational tools, but the same core physics governs safe descent through Earth’s atmosphere.

3.  Human Verification of Computer Calculations
What Already Existed
Early IBM mainframe computers were already in development.
What She Helped Pioneer
Before the launch of Friendship 7, astronaut John Glenn requested that Johnson manually verify the computer’s orbital calculations.
She did not invent computer systems — but she played a key role during the transition from human computation to machine computation in aerospace.
This moment represents early operational trust-building between humans and automated systems.
How It Shows Up Today
Modern aerospace missions still use independent verification and validation processes. NASA operates the Katherine Johnson Independent Verification and Validation Facility to evaluate mission software safety and reliability.

4.  Orbital Rendezvous Mathematics
What Already Existed
Celestial mechanics and boundary-value orbital problems were studied before the Space Age.
What She Helped Pioneer
Johnson co-authored early NASA technical reports on orbital rendezvous — the mathematics required for two spacecraft moving at high speed to meet in orbit.
At the time, this was new territory for crewed space missions.
How It Shows Up Today
Spacecraft docking with the International Space Station and future lunar missions still rely on refined versions of rendezvous equations. Modern tools solve problems such as Lambert's problem computationally, but the governing principles are unchanged.

5.        Institutional & Educational Legacy
What Already Existed
Mathematics and aerospace engineering were established disciplines.
What She Helped Pioneer
